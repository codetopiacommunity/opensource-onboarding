# What Is Open Source?

Before you type a single command, it is worth knowing what you are
signing up for and why it is worth your time.

This guide is different from all the others: there is nothing to
install and nothing to type. Just read. It takes about five minutes,
and everything after it will make more sense because of it.

---
## You already use open source every day

You may have never heard the term before, but you have been using
open source software for years without knowing it.

- **Firefox**, the web browser, is open source.
- **VS Code**, the most popular code editor in the world, is open
  source.
- **Linux** runs most of the servers behind the websites you visit
  every day, and it is the foundation of Android.
- **Wikipedia** runs on open source software written by volunteers.

Open source is not a niche corner of the software world. It is the
plumbing the entire internet runs on, and most of it is built and
maintained by people collaborating in the open, many of them
volunteers, from everywhere in the world.

---
## So what does "open source" actually mean?

Most software is closed. You can use it, but you cannot see how it
works inside, and you certainly cannot change it.

Open source software is the opposite. The code is public. Anyone can
read it, learn from it, use it, and, this is the part that matters for
you, **anyone can help improve it**. You do not need to apply for a
job. You do not need anyone's permission to get started. If you spot
something that could be better and you are willing to do the work, you
can contribute, and your improvement ships to every user of that
project.

Every open source project has:

- **Maintainers**: the people responsible for the project. They review
  incoming changes and decide what gets included.
- **Contributors**: everyone who improves the project, whether once or
  regularly. By the end of this course, that will be you.

## The life of one contribution

Here is how contributing actually works, start to finish.

<a href="https://portal.community.codetopia.org/@rosemaryboahemaa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Rosemary</a>
is reading the documentation of a project she uses and notices the
install instructions are missing a step. She is new. She has never
contributed to anything.

1. She opens an **issue**, a public note on the project saying "the
   install docs are missing a step." That alone is already a
   contribution.
2. A maintainer replies: "Good catch. Want to fix it?"
3. Rosemary makes her own copy of the project, called a **fork**, fixes
   the docs on her copy, and submits the change back as a **pull
   request**, a proposal that says "here is my improvement, please
   pull it into the project."
4. A maintainer **reviews** her change, suggests one small tweak, and
   Rosemary updates it.
5. The maintainer **merges** it. Rosemary's fix is now part of the
   project. Every future user who reads those docs benefits from her
   work, and her name is permanently in the project's history.

That is the entire loop. Issues, forks, pull requests, reviews,
merges. It probably sounds like jargon right now. That is fine. This
course walks you through doing every one of those steps yourself, for
real, at a comfortable pace.

---
## Why is this worth your time?

**It is real collaboration experience.** Open source is how software
is built by teams in the real world: proposing changes, having your
work reviewed, giving feedback on someone else's work, communicating
clearly in writing with people in other time zones. These are exactly
the skills professional developers use every day, and open source
lets you build them without anyone needing to hire you first.

**Your work is public and permanent.** Every contribution you make is
attached to your name, visible to anyone, forever. Many developers
have been offered jobs because someone saw their open source work.
Your contribution history becomes a portfolio you never have to write.

**You learn faster with other people.** Working alone, you do not
know what you do not know. In open source, experienced people review
your work and tell you, kindly and specifically, how to make it
better. That feedback loop is the fastest way to grow.

**It feels good.** You use software built by volunteers every day.
Contributing is how you give some of that back, and there is a real
community on the other side of it.

---
## You do not have to be a programmer

This one matters, so read it twice.

Some of the most valued contributions to open source projects are not
code: fixing documentation, reporting bugs clearly, translating
projects into other languages, improving designs, helping triage
issues, answering questions from other newcomers. Rosemary's
contribution in the story above was documentation, and it was just as
real as any code change.

If you can spot a problem and communicate clearly, you have something
open source needs.

## Before you start: you will get stuck

Not might. Will. Everyone does, including people who have been doing
this for years. A command will fail, or an error will appear that means
nothing to you. That is not a sign you are not cut out for this. It is
the normal texture of the work.

So here is the plan for when it happens:

1. Every guide has a **Stuck?** section at the bottom listing what
   commonly goes wrong at that step, with the fix.
2. If that does not cover it, [Getting Help](../HELP.md) explains where
   to ask and how to ask so you get a useful answer quickly.

Read [Getting Help](../HELP.md) now, before you need it. It takes two
minutes and it means you will not be hunting for it while frustrated.
